OBD-II plug-in vs hard-wired trackers: which is right for your fleet?

How the two main types of aftermarket vehicle tracker differ in installation, reliability, data, security and cost, with a decision table and a checklist for choosing.

Comparison guide · By the Fleet Safety Notes editorial team · Last reviewed November 2026 · About 8 minutes

When you add tracking to vans or cars that did not come with it, you will usually choose between two kinds of device: a small unit that plugs into the vehicle's diagnostic socket, or a unit that a technician wires into the vehicle's electrical system and hides behind the dashboard. Both report location, trips and driving events to the same kind of software. The differences are in how they are fitted, how easily they can be removed, what data they can read, and what they cost over the life of a contract. This guide sets those differences out so you can pick the right type for each part of your fleet. If you are new to the subject, our beginner's guide to fleet telematics explains how tracking systems work end to end.

The two types in plain terms

Plug-in (OBD) trackers

A plug-in tracker is a small box, often called a "dongle", that pushes into the vehicle's on-board diagnostics (OBD) socket, the same socket a garage uses to read fault codes. It takes power from the socket and can usually read some vehicle data through it. Fitting takes minutes and needs no tools. Some units connect directly to the socket; others use a short extension or "Y" cable so the unit can be tucked out of sight and the socket left free for a mechanic.

Hard-wired trackers

A hard-wired tracker is connected by a technician to the vehicle's permanent power, ignition feed and earth, and mounted out of view. Depending on the product, it may also be wired to the vehicle's data network or to extra inputs such as a driver ID reader, a door sensor, a power take-off switch or a temperature probe. Fitting typically takes longer than a plug-in, and most providers use their own or approved installers.

A third option is telematics built in by the vehicle manufacturer, which needs no aftermarket device at all. We cover it in our telematics guide's section on device types. This page compares the two aftermarket options.

A quick note on the diagnostic port

In the United States, the California Air Resources Board explains that OBD II applies to all 1996 and newer petrol and alternative-fuel passenger cars and trucks, and to diesel passenger cars and trucks from 1997, and that the US EPA applies its own OBD requirements to 1996 and newer cars and light trucks sold in every state. A joint public service announcement from the FBI and the US road safety regulator NHTSA describes OBD-II as a standardised diagnostics port found on modern vehicles.

In Europe, Directive 98/69/EC (an EU law, since repealed and replaced, which also formed the basis of UK rules) required on-board diagnostic systems on petrol cars and light vans, and phased them in for diesel cars from 2003 for new types and 2004 for all types, with diesel light vans following in 2005 and 2006 depending on their weight class. In practice this means most cars and vans in a modern small fleet have a diagnostic socket that a plug-in tracker can use. Heavy trucks are different, and are covered below.

Even with a standard socket, the amount of data a plug-in device can read, such as fuel level, odometer or fault codes, varies by make, model and the provider's software. Always ask the provider to confirm support for your exact vehicles.

Neither type is "better" overall. They suit different vehicles and different goals, and many fleets use both.

When a plug-in tracker is the better fit

  • Short-term or changing vehicles. Hire vehicles, short leases, or a fleet that turns over often. Moving a plug-in unit costs nothing in technician time.
  • A trial. Testing a provider on two or three vehicles before committing is far easier when nobody has to wire anything in. Our guide to GPS tracking costs and contracts explains why a trial is worth insisting on.
  • Grey fleet or mixed-use cars, where drilling and wiring someone's own or a leased car is not acceptable. Take extra care with privacy here; see the security and privacy section below.
  • Driving-style insight on light vehicles, such as speeding and harsh braking alerts that feed into driver risk scores, where theft recovery is not the main goal.

The main weakness is that the unit is easy to remove. If drivers resist tracking, expect some units to be unplugged. Choose a system that alerts you when a device loses power or stops reporting, and agree in your vehicle policy that removing it is not allowed.

When a hard-wired tracker is the better fit

  • High-value vehicles or loads, where stolen-vehicle tracking matters. A concealed unit, ideally with its own backup battery, keeps reporting after a thief has found and removed anything obvious.
  • Vehicles kept for several years, where the one-off installation cost is spread over a long life.
  • Extra inputs, such as driver identification fobs, refrigeration temperature, tipper or crane use, or door opening on cash and parcel vans.
  • Trailers, plant and older vehicles without a usable diagnostic socket.
  • Where tampering is a known problem. A hidden unit is much harder to disconnect without leaving a trace.
Wiring into a modern vehicle's electrics should be done by a competent installer. Before fitting, check your vehicle warranty, lease agreement and insurer's requirements, and ask the provider to confirm in writing who is responsible if the installation causes an electrical fault. This is general information, not legal advice.

Security and privacy

A device that plugs into the diagnostic socket sits on the vehicle's internal network and, because it also has a mobile or Bluetooth connection, can create a route in from outside. The 2016 FBI and NHTSA announcement warned that third-party devices plugged into the diagnostic port, such as insurance dongles and telematics tools, could add wireless vulnerabilities. It advised owners to check the security and privacy policies of device makers and to avoid connecting unknown or untrusted devices to the port. Hard-wired units that connect to the vehicle data network raise similar questions.

Whichever type you choose, ask the provider:

  1. Does the device only read data, or can it send commands to the vehicle?
  2. Is data encrypted between the device and the server?
  3. How are device software (firmware) updates delivered, and how often?
  4. Can Bluetooth or other local connections be switched off if you do not use them?
  5. How does the provider handle reported security flaws?

Tracking data about identifiable drivers is also personal data under UK and EU data protection law. The UK Information Commissioner's Office expects employers to tell workers about monitoring and to keep it proportionate to a clear purpose, and Ireland's Data Protection Commission treats employee vehicle tracking as high risk. The device type does not change those duties, but a plug-in unit fitted to a car that is also used privately makes a private-use mode and clear rules especially important. Heavy trucks and US ELDs

Heavy goods vehicles generally use a different diagnostic connector and data network from cars and vans, so a car-style OBD-II dongle will not simply fit. Truck tracking is usually either hard-wired or connected to the truck's own diagnostic connector with a dedicated cable.

In the US, this matters for electronic logging devices (ELDs). The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration (FMCSA) requires an ELD to be integrally synchronised with the engine, meaning it monitors the engine to capture power status, vehicle motion, miles driven and engine hours, and says GPS alone cannot substitute for the required engine data. FMCSA's technical FAQ says a physical connection between the engine control module and the ELD is not required, and gives hard-wiring to the J1939 plug and Bluetooth connectivity as examples of how the two may connect. FMCSA's comparison of older recording devices and ELDs notes that vehicles older than model year 2000 are exempt from the engine-synchronisation requirement. If an ELD is part of your plan, check that the provider's device is on FMCSA's registered list and works with your trucks before you choose a tracker type.

Checklist for choosing a device type

  • List each vehicle with make, model, year, ownership (owned, leased, hired) and expected time in the fleet
  • Confirm the provider supports data from your exact vehicles through the diagnostic socket
  • Decide whether theft recovery is a goal for each vehicle
  • List any extra inputs you need (driver ID, PTO, temperature, doors)
  • Check vehicle warranty, lease and insurer terms before any wiring
  • Get installation, removal and swap-out charges in writing
  • Ask for tamper or power-loss alerts and test them during the trial
  • Ask the five security questions above and keep the answers
  • Plan how you will inform drivers and set data retention
  • For US trucks needing ELDs, confirm FMCSA registration and engine compatibility
  • Trial both types on a few vehicles before rolling out
