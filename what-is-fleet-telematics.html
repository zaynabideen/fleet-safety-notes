What is fleet telematics? A beginner's guide for small fleets

How telematics systems work, what data they collect, what they can do for a small fleet, and the questions to ask before you buy one.

"Telematics" combines telecommunications and informatics: sending data from a vehicle to somewhere it can be used. In a fleet, a telematics system tells you where your vehicles are, how they are being driven and how healthy they are, usually through a web dashboard and a mobile app. For a small fleet, it is often the first piece of technology that turns guesswork into numbers.

How telematics works

  1. A device in the vehicle collects data from a GPS receiver, motion sensors (accelerometers) and, in most systems, the vehicle's own computer through the diagnostic port or data bus.
  2. A mobile data connection (usually 4G or 5G) sends that data to the provider's servers every few seconds or minutes.
  3. Software turns the raw data into maps, alerts, reports and scores that managers can act on.

Many systems also connect to dash cams, temperature sensors, door sensors or driver ID tags, so all vehicle data sits in one place.

What telematics tracks

DataWhat it tells you
Location and route historyWhere vehicles are now, where they have been, arrival times

The three main device types

  • Plug-in devices fit into the vehicle's diagnostic port (OBD-II in cars and light vans). They take minutes to install and are easy to move between vehicles, but they are also easy for a driver to unplug.
  • Hard-wired devices are installed behind the dashboard by a technician. They are more tamper-resistant and suit vans, trucks and plant equipment.
  • Manufacturer-embedded telematics is built into many newer vehicles at the factory. It needs no extra hardware, but data access, features and subscription terms depend on the manufacturer, which matters if your fleet has mixed brands.

What telematics can do for a small fleet

  • Lower fuel costs. Reducing idling, speeding and harsh driving cuts fuel use. Try our fuel cost calculator to see what a small improvement is worth.
  • Safer driving. Event data shows which habits need coaching, and pairs naturally with fleet dash cams.
  • Better maintenance. Mileage and fault-code alerts let you service vehicles on time and catch problems before a breakdown.
  • Customer service. Accurate locations mean better arrival estimates and proof of visit.
  • Theft recovery. Live location helps police find a stolen vehicle, and some insurers recognise tracking devices.
  • Less admin. Automatic mileage and timesheet reports replace paper logs.

Telematics and compliance

In some places, part of a telematics system is a legal requirement rather than an option. In the United States, the electronic logging device (ELD) rule requires many commercial drivers to record hours of service electronically. An ELD syncs with the vehicle's engine to record driving time automatically. The ELD rule did not change the underlying hours-of-service limits; it changed how they are recorded.

Heavy goods vehicles in the UK and European Union use tachographs for the same purpose. Rules differ by country, vehicle weight and type of operation, so always check the regulator in each country where you operate.

Driver privacy: get this right from day one

Telematics data is linked to identifiable drivers, so data protection law usually applies: GDPR in the UK and EU, and a growing set of state and national privacy laws elsewhere. Good practice everywhere includes:

  • Telling drivers in writing what is tracked, when and why, before the system goes live
  • Collecting only what you need, and keeping it only as long as you need it
  • Offering a privacy mode for permitted personal use outside working hours, if vehicles are used privately
  • Restricting who in the business can see the data
This is general information, not legal advice. Requirements vary by country and region, so check local rules before installing tracking.

How to choose a telematics system

  1. Start with your problem. Fuel, safety, customer service and compliance need different features. Pay for what solves yours.
  2. Check device fit. Will it work in every vehicle you run, including older models and mixed brands?
  3. Ask about contract terms. Monthly fee per vehicle, contract length, hardware ownership, and the cost of adding or removing vehicles.
  4. Test the software. Ask for a trial on two or three vehicles. If managers find the dashboard confusing, the data will not get used.
  5. Check data access. Can you export your data, and connect it to the dash cams, fuel cards or payroll tools you already use?
  6. Plan the rollout. Explain the system to drivers, focus early reports on coaching rather than discipline, and review results after the first few months.
