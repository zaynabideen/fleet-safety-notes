Driver risk scores explained: how fleet safety scoring works

What goes into a driver risk score, why raw event counts mislead, a worked example you can copy, and how to use scores to coach drivers rather than punish them.

Almost every telematics or dash cam platform gives each driver a safety score. Managers use it to decide who needs coaching, and sometimes insurers ask for it. But the number is only useful if you understand how it was built. Two platforms can give the same driver very different scores, and a careless reading of either can lead to the wrong driver getting the blame.

What a risk score actually is

A risk score is a summary of risky driving events, weighted by how dangerous each one is, and scaled by how much the driver drove. Some platforms show it as "points" where lower is better; others flip it into a score out of 100 where higher is better. The underlying idea is the same.

The events that feed the score

Events come from two sources: the vehicle's accelerometer and GPS (telematics), and the camera's AI.

  • Telematics events: harsh braking, harsh acceleration, harsh cornering, speeding against the posted limit, excessive idling.
  • Camera AI events: phone use, following too closely, no seatbelt, drowsiness or distraction, running a stop sign or red light.

Each event type gets a weight. A single phone-use event usually counts for much more than a single harsh brake, because the crash risk is far higher.

Event quality matters. A harsh-braking event caused by another driver pulling out is not the same as one caused by tailgating. Good platforms let a reviewer dismiss events that were not the driver's fault, and video is what makes that possible.

Why scores must be per 1,000 miles

Raw event counts punish your busiest drivers. Someone who drives 1,500 miles a week will naturally collect more events than someone who drives 200. So scores are normalised, usually to points per 1,000 miles (or per 100 hours of driving). That way you compare how a person drives, not how much.

A worked example

Here is a simple scoring model using illustrative weights. Real platforms use their own weights and often more event types, but the arithmetic is the same.

Driver A has more than three times as many points, so at first glance looks like the problem. Once you adjust for distance, Driver B is actually the riskier driver per mile. This is exactly the mistake that raw leaderboards lead managers into.

Notice too that Driver A's single phone-use event is worth almost as much as six harsh brakes. That is deliberate: weights should reflect real crash risk, not how often an event happens.

Look at the trend, not one week

One bad week can be bad luck: a new route, roadworks, an unusually busy shift. A useful score looks back over several weeks and asks whether the driver is improving, stable or getting worse. A practical approach is to compare the last four weeks with the four weeks before, and flag drivers whose points per 1,000 miles have risen sharply, as well as those with a consistently high level.

Also check how much data sits behind a score. A driver with only 80 miles in the period can swing wildly from one event. Many fleets set a minimum distance before a score is shown at all.

How to use scores fairly

  • Compare like with like. Urban multi-drop routes produce more harsh braking than motorway trunking. Compare drivers on similar work.
  • Coach with the video. Showing a driver the actual clip is far more effective than showing them a number, and lets them explain context.
  • Recognise improvement. Drivers who improve should hear about it. Scores used only for discipline quickly lose drivers' trust.
  • Be transparent. Tell drivers which events are scored and how. Under UK GDPR they have a right to understand how monitoring data about them is used.

Scores are a starting point for a conversation, not a verdict. Used that way, they are one of the most effective tools a small fleet has for reducing incidents. If you are still choosing hardware, start with the dash cam buying guide.
