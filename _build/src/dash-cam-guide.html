How to choose a dash cam for a small fleet

A practical checklist for fleets of 2 to 50 vehicles, wherever you operate: which camera type you need, which features are worth paying for, and the legal and cost questions most buyers forget.

Buying guide · By the Fleet Safety Notes editorial team · Last reviewed November 2026 · About 9 minutes

A fleet dash cam does two jobs. It protects you after an incident, with footage that shows what really happened. And, if you choose the right system, it helps prevent the next incident by showing you which driving habits need coaching. Most small fleets buy for the first job and only later discover how valuable the second one is. This guide helps you plan for both from the start.

1. Pick the camera type first

TypeWhat it recordsBest for
Forward-facingThe road aheadProving fault in front collisions; the minimum for any fleet
Dual-facingRoad ahead and the driver/cabDetecting phone use, distraction and fatigue; defending drivers against false claims
Multi-camera / 360°Front, sides, rear, sometimes cabVans and HGVs with blind spots, reversing incidents, cyclist and pedestrian risk in cities

If you only run cars on motorways, forward-facing may be enough. If your drivers do multi-drop work in towns, side and rear coverage often pays for itself in avoided low-speed damage disputes.

2. The features that actually matter

  • Night and glare performance. Resolution numbers on the box mean little if number plates are unreadable at night or into a low sun. Ask for sample footage filmed at night and in rain.
  • Connectivity. SD-card-only cameras are cheap but you must physically retrieve the card, and footage can be overwritten before you get to it. 4G cameras upload incident clips automatically, so you see a collision within minutes.
  • Event detection. A g-sensor that locks footage on harsh braking or impact is essential. Without it, the clip you need may be recorded over.
  • Retention and retrieval. Check how long continuous footage is kept on the device, and whether you can request a clip remotely after the fact, for example when a third party makes a claim days later.
  • Integration. If you already use telematics, a camera that shares the same dashboard saves time and lets you see video alongside speed and location.

3. AI alerts: useful, but check the quality

Modern fleet cameras use AI to spot risky behaviour: following too closely, phone use, not wearing a seatbelt, rolling through stop lines, drowsiness. These alerts are where coaching value comes from, but they are only as good as the footage the AI sees.

Many false alerts have simple, preventable causes: a dirty or misaligned lens, strong glare, a camera mounted at the wrong angle, or a driver-facing lens partly blocked by a sun visor or air freshener. Build lens cleaning and mounting checks into routine vehicle inspections. A system that raises a flood of false alerts quickly gets ignored by managers and resented by drivers.

Ask every vendor: Are AI alerts reviewed by a human before they reach me? Can I give feedback when an alert is wrong? Can I turn individual alert types on and off?

4. Installation and windscreen rules

Hard-wired installation is more reliable than a 12V socket plug, because the camera keeps recording and parking-mode protection works without a driver remembering to plug it in. Most fleet vendors offer professional fitting.

Placement matters for safety and for roadworthiness checks. Most countries restrict anything that blocks the driver's view through the windscreen, so mount cameras high, behind or beside the rear-view mirror, and check local rules. Two examples:

  • United States (commercial vehicles): federal rules allow vehicle safety technology on the windshield only within set zones: no more than 8.5 inches below the upper edge, or 7 inches above the lower edge, of the area swept by the wipers, and outside the driver's sight lines to the road, signs and signals.
  • United Kingdom: the MOT inspection manual treats the area swept by the wipers as critical, and defines "zone A" as a 290mm-wide strip centred on the steering wheel, where the rules are strictest. An obstruction is a defect when it significantly affects the driver's view.

Ask your fitter if you are unsure, and keep the rules of every country your vehicles operate in.

5. Data protection: tell your drivers before you record them

Fleet footage usually contains personal data: your drivers, other road users, sometimes audio. In the UK and EU, GDPR applies, and regulators such as the UK's Information Commissioner's Office (ICO) expect employers to be open about monitoring. In the US, rules vary by state, and some states require everyone's consent before a conversation is recorded, which matters for in-cab audio. Wherever you operate, good practice is the same:

  • Tell drivers in writing what is recorded, when, why, and who can view it.
  • Driver-facing cameras and audio recording are more intrusive. Carry out a data protection impact assessment (DPIA) before you roll them out, and consider whether audio is really needed.
  • Set a retention period and delete footage you no longer need.
  • Consider window stickers telling the public that recording is in use.
This is general information, not legal advice. Check the rules where you operate, or speak to an adviser, before introducing in-cab cameras.

6. Work out the true cost per vehicle

The camera price is often the smallest part of the bill. Before comparing quotes, put every option into the same shape:

  1. Hardware cost per vehicle (or whether it is included in the contract)
  2. Installation cost per vehicle
  3. Monthly subscription per vehicle, including data
  4. Contract length and what happens when it ends: do you keep the hardware?
  5. Cost to add or remove vehicles mid-contract

Multiply out to a total over the contract term per vehicle. Then ask your insurer whether they offer a premium discount for camera-equipped fleets. Some do, and it can offset part of the subscription.

Quick checklist before you sign

  • I have seen night and rain sample footage from this exact model.
  • Incident clips upload automatically, and I can retrieve older footage remotely.
  • AI alerts can be tuned, and false alerts can be reported.
  • Professional, hard-wired installation is included or priced.
  • I have told drivers in writing and completed a DPIA if using driver-facing cameras.
  • I know the total cost per vehicle over the full contract.
