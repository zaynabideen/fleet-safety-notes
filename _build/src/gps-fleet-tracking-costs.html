GPS fleet tracking costs: contracts and hidden fees explained

What you actually pay for when you buy vehicle tracking, the contract terms that decide the real cost, and a simple worksheet for comparing quotes fairly.

The headline price for GPS fleet tracking is usually a monthly fee per vehicle. That figure is rarely the whole story. Hardware, installation, contract length, add-ons, early-exit terms and the cost of replacing devices when mobile networks change can all move the real cost a long way from the number on the brochure. This guide explains each cost so you can ask the right questions before you sign. If you are new to the technology itself, start with our beginner's guide to fleet telematics.

We do not publish provider prices. Prices change often, vary by country and fleet size, and are usually negotiated. The figures in the worked example below are made up purely to show the method.

What you actually pay for

Almost every tracking quote is built from the same five parts. Some providers bundle them together; others list each separately.

Cost partWhat it coversQuestions to ask
HardwareThe tracking unit, plus any harnesses, antennas, driver ID readers or sensorsDo we buy it, lease it, or is it "free" inside the subscription? Who owns it at the end?
InstallationFitting by a technician (hard-wired units) or self-fit (plug-in units)Is there a charge per vehicle, per visit, or for travel? What about removal and refitting when we change vehicles?
SubscriptionMobile data, the software platform, the app and standard supportIs the price per vehicle or per device? Which features are included at this tier?
Add-onsExtra modules such as dash cam video, fuel card integration, temperature sensors, route planning, or hours-of-service toolsWhich features we saw in the demo are extras? Are add-ons on the same contract term?
Ending the contractEarly termination charges, device returns and data exportWhat do we pay if we leave early, sell vehicles or shrink the fleet?

The main pricing models

Hardware bundled into the subscription

The device appears free or heavily discounted, and its cost is recovered through a longer fixed contract. This keeps upfront spending low, which suits small fleets with tight cash flow. The trade-off is commitment: you are usually locked in for the full term, and the device often stays the provider's property.

Hardware bought upfront, shorter or rolling subscription

You pay more on day one but usually get more freedom: shorter terms, or month-to-month plans. Check whether the device is locked to that provider's platform. If it is, owning the hardware gives you less freedom than it sounds, because it may be useless if you switch.

Leased hardware

A separate lease or rental charge for the device sits alongside the software fee. Read both agreements, because their terms, end dates and cancellation rules may differ.

Manufacturer-embedded telematics

Many newer vans and trucks have a built-in connectivity unit, and some fleet platforms can read data from it without extra hardware. This can remove installation costs, but data access, the features available and subscription terms depend on the vehicle maker and the platform, so check what works for each brand in a mixed fleet. For a comparison of device types, see our guide on plug-in, hard-wired and embedded devices.

Contract terms that decide the real cost

For a small fleet, the contract matters as much as the monthly fee. These are the clauses worth reading line by line:

  • Term length. Multi-year terms are common in fleet tracking. A lower monthly rate on a longer term is not automatically cheaper if your fleet is likely to change.
  • Automatic renewal. Many business contracts renew for another full term unless you give written notice within a set window before the end date. Put that notice date in your calendar the day you sign.
  • Minimum vehicle numbers. Some contracts commit you to paying for a set number of units for the whole term, even if you sell vehicles.
  • Price changes. Look for clauses that allow annual price increases, and whether there is a cap.
  • Adding vehicles. Does each new vehicle start its own full-length term? This can leave you with many end dates that never line up, making it hard to leave.
  • Early termination. Find out how the charge is worked out. Some contracts require you to pay the remaining months in full.
  • Service levels. What happens if devices fail, the platform is down, or support is slow? Is a faulty unit replaced free, and who pays for the technician visit?
Consumer protections such as cooling-off periods often do not apply to business contracts. Do not assume you can cancel easily because you could with a personal phone contract. This is general information, not legal advice.

Hidden fees to ask about

None of these are hidden if you ask in writing. They become "hidden" when nobody asks until the invoice arrives.

  • Activation or set-up fees per device or per account.
  • Swap-out fees for moving a tracker from a vehicle you sold to its replacement.
  • Return or non-return charges for devices at the end of the contract, including the cost of a technician to remove hard-wired units.
  • Cross-border data charges if vehicles regularly travel to other countries.
  • Extra users or seats on the software for managers, dispatchers or accountants.
  • Video storage limits on connected dash cams, with charges for extra cloud storage or longer retention. Our dash cam buying guide covers video costs in more detail.
  • Premium reports and integrations, such as access to an API or a link to payroll or fuel card systems.
  • Paper invoices or non-direct-debit payment surcharges.
  • Hardware upgrades forced by mobile network changes, explained next.

Network switch-offs: the cost nobody budgets for

Trackers send data over mobile networks. When an operator switches off an old network generation, devices that rely only on it stop reporting and must be replaced. This has already happened to 3G in several of our readers' countries, and 2G is next in the UK.

CountryStatus (as reported by the official source)
United StatesThe major carriers shut down their 3G networks during 2022, according to the FCC's consumer guide. Vehicle SOS services were among the devices named as affected.
United KingdomOfcom reports that all four mobile network operators have completed their 3G switch-off. 2G will follow: Ofcom lists EE beginning from May 2029, O2 starting in summer 2029 and the Vodafone 2G network during 2030, with 2033 as the latest date. Ofcom names telemetry and tracker devices among those that may be affected.
AustraliaThe Australian Government reports that TPG Telecom switched off 3G in January 2024, and Telstra and Optus in November 2024.

Before you sign, ask the provider:

  1. Which network technology does the device use (4G, LTE-M, 5G), and does it fall back to 2G?
  2. Does it use a roaming SIM? Ofcom notes that changes to roaming access can affect devices that rely on another operator's network.
  3. If a network switch-off makes the device obsolete during the contract, who pays for the replacement unit and the installation visit?

Get the answer to the third question in the contract, not just in an email from a salesperson.

Your data: access, export and switching

Years of trip history, mileage records and driver scores have real value, for example in an insurance dispute or a tax enquiry. Ask whether you can export all your data in a standard format, whether that costs extra, and how long the provider keeps it after you leave.

In the European Union, the Data Act has applied since 12 September 2025. According to the European Commission's explainer, users of connected products, which include connected cars, have a right to access the data the product generates, free of charge, and to share it with a third party. The Act also phases out charges for switching between cloud data processing services, with switching charges, including data egress fees, removed from 12 January 2027. How these rules apply to a particular tracking service depends on the details, so ask the provider how it meets them. Outside the EU, your rights depend on local law and, mostly, on your contract.

Tracking data about identifiable drivers is also personal data under GDPR in the UK and EU. The UK Information Commissioner's Office expects employers to tell workers about monitoring, use the least intrusive approach that achieves a clear purpose, keep only relevant data, and carry out a data protection impact assessment where monitoring is likely to be high risk. Ireland's Data Protection Commission says vehicle tracking carries a high privacy risk for employees and should not be used for general monitoring of staff. Budget time for this work, and check that the system supports privacy features such as a private-use mode and set retention periods.

How to compare quotes: a worked example

Quotes are hard to compare because providers bundle costs differently. The fix is to work out the total cost over the contract and divide it by the number of vehicle-months. The example below uses invented numbers for a fleet of 10 vans.

Item (illustrative only)Quote A: bundled, 36 monthsQuote B: buy device, 12 months
Hardware010 × 100 = 1,000
Installation10 × 50 = 50010 × 50 = 500
Subscription10 × 20 × 36 = 7,20010 × 25 × 12 = 3,000
Add-on needed (driver ID)10 × 3 × 36 = 1,080Included
Total over term8,7804,500
Vehicle-months10 × 36 = 36010 × 12 = 120
Cost per vehicle per month24.3937.50

On these made-up figures, Quote A works out cheaper per vehicle per month, even though its headline fee only looked slightly lower. Quote B costs more per month but leaves you free after a year, and if it renewed at the same subscription with no new hardware or installation, the second year would cost only the 25 per vehicle per month. Now test each quote against a realistic change: if you expect to sell three vans in year two, work out what Quote A's minimum-vehicle and early-termination clauses would charge for those three units. That one calculation often decides the choice.

Before-you-sign checklist

  • Written quote showing hardware, installation, subscription and every add-on separately
  • Total cost over the full term and cost per vehicle per month calculated
  • Contract length, renewal notice period and notice date diarised
  • Rules for adding, removing and swapping vehicles, and whether each starts a new term
  • Early termination calculation in writing
  • Price increase clause and any cap
  • Network technology confirmed, and who pays if a switch-off makes devices obsolete
  • Hardware ownership and end-of-contract return or removal costs
  • Data export format, cost and retention after you leave
  • Privacy features (private mode, retention settings) and a plan to inform drivers
  • A trial on two or three vehicles before committing the whole fleet
